The teas

Six kinds of Japanese tea, and what each one is actually like.

All of it is one plant, Camellia sinensis. What separates these teas is shade, steaming, roasting, and which part of the plant is used. Fifty-one teas sit behind these six headings; tell me what you pour and I will point you at the right one.

Sencha

Steamed · unshaded · 22 in the caddie

Japan’s everyday tea and about eighty per cent of what the country grows. Leaves are steamed within hours of picking to stop oxidation, then rolled into dark needles. Expect a bright, grassy cup with real sweetness underneath and a clean astringent edge. The range is enormous: light-steamed asamushi from the Shizuoka hills tastes crisp and aromatic, while deep-steamed fukamushi from Kagoshima pours cloudy, thick and forgiving — the better choice behind a busy counter.

Water
70–80°C
Time
60 seconds
Leaf
5 g / 300 ml
Infusions
Two to three

Gyokuro

Shade-grown · jade dew

For about three weeks before harvest the gardens are covered, and the plant responds by making more theanine and less catechin. The result is Japan’s most concentrated tea: deep, dark needles that brew a small, thick, savoury cup closer to broth than to what most Americans mean by green tea. Brew it cool and slow, in a small pot, and drink it in mouthfuls rather than by the mug. This is the tea I use to change the mind of someone who is certain they dislike green tea.

Water
50–60°C
Time
2 minutes
Leaf
10 g / 180 ml
Infusions
Three

Matcha

Stone-milled tencha · whole leaf

Shaded leaf, steamed and dried without rolling — that is tencha — then ground on granite mills slowly enough not to scorch it. Because the leaf is drunk rather than infused, matcha is thicker, sweeter and more vivid than any steeped tea, and its colour is the first honest test of quality: a dull olive powder was milled from lesser leaf or has been open too long. I carry ceremonial grades for the bowl and culinary grades for lattes and pastry, and I choose them for how they behave in a café, not for how they score on paper.

Water
80°C
Method
Whisked
Powder
2 g / 70 ml
Sift first
Always

Hojicha

Roasted · low caffeine

Invented in 1920s Kyoto out of leaf nobody wanted, and now the tea I sell most easily. Later-harvest leaves and stems are fired until they turn reddish-brown, which burns off the grassiness and most of the caffeine and leaves toasted grain, caramel and a little cocoa. It brews amber, has almost no astringency, forgives boiling water and a long steep, and works hot, iced or with milk. Serve it after dinner, or to anyone who says tea keeps them awake.

Water
95–100°C
Time
30–45 sec
Leaf
6 g / 300 ml
Also good
Iced, with milk

Genmaicha

Green tea with roasted rice

Sencha or bancha blended roughly half and half with roasted brown rice, some of which pops white in the roasting. It began as a way to stretch expensive leaf and stayed because people love it: toasted, nutty, faintly of popcorn, with the green tea still legible underneath. The rice dilutes the caffeine and softens the astringency, which makes it the most food-friendly tea I carry — particularly good against fried food, rice dishes and anything salty.

Water
80–85°C
Time
45–60 sec
Leaf
5 g / 300 ml
Serves
With food

Kukicha

Stems & stalks · twig tea

The pale stems and stalks sorted out while finishing sencha and gyokuro, kept and sold on their own. Because stems carry the plant’s sweetness rather than its bitterness, kukicha is light, creamy and quietly sweet, with a fresh green aroma and very little astringency. Made from gyokuro stems it is called karigane, and it is one of the loveliest and least known teas in the caddie — the one I drink myself in the afternoon.

Water
70–80°C
Time
60–90 sec
Leaf
5 g / 300 ml
Caffeine
Low
